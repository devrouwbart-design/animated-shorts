In nineteen eighty, an entire lake went down the drain in just a few hours, and it took an oil rig, a tugboat and eleven barges with it. You see, Lake Peigneur in Louisiana is only ten feet deep. Calm, full of fish, with a fancy garden on the shore. And right in the middle, a Texaco rig is drilling for oil. Twelve hundred feet down, the drill gets stuck. Then the whole rig starts to tilt. The crew hears loud pops, jumps in a boat, and gets away just in time. Behind them, the lake starts to spin. A whirlpool swallows the rig. Then a tugboat. Then the barges, one after another. A fisherman floors his tiny boat and makes it to shore. Even the canal to the sea turns around and pours in backwards, as a waterfall. Wait. Where is all that water going? Right under the lake was a salt mine, with fifty-five miners inside. An electrician spots water rushing down a tunnel and sounds the alarm. Luckily, every single miner gets out. Unluckily, the lake doesn't. The drill had punched straight through the roof of the mine. Picture a bathtub. Pull the plug, and the water swirls down the drain. But this drain was made of salt. And water dissolves salt. So the faster the lake drained, the bigger the drain got. It took two days for the canal to fill the lake back up. And then, one by one, nine of the barges popped back up like corks. The lake came back too. Just saltier.
