Three astronauts were saved from suffocating in space by duct tape, a plastic bag and a sock.
You see, in April nineteen seventy, Apollo thirteen is two hundred thousand miles from Earth, on its way to the Moon. Then, bang. An oxygen tank explodes. Alarms everywhere. And the radio crackles: "Houston, we've had a problem."
The main ship is dying. So the three astronauts squeeze into the lunar lander, a tiny craft built for two people for two days. They need it for three, for four days.
Oxygen? Enough. The problem is what they breathe out. Carbon dioxide. With every breath, the meter creeps up.
The main ship has spare air filters. But they're square, and the lander's holder is round.
It's like landing abroad and finding your phone charger doesn't fit the wall socket. Except up here, you don't run out of battery. You run out of air.
Down in Houston, engineers with crew cuts and pocket protectors pile up only what the crew has on board. Plastic bags. Cardboard. Space suit hoses. A sock. And lots of duct tape.
They invent a box that hooks the square filter to a round hose, and read the instructions up by radio, step by step. An hour later, the crew has built it: the mailbox.
The carbon dioxide drops to almost nothing. All three make it home.
And that famous line, "Houston, we have a problem"? Nobody ever said it. The real words were "Houston, we've had a problem." Past tense. Thanks to a sock, it stayed that way.
