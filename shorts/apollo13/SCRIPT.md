# The Square Peg That Saved Apollo 13

Original 55-70 s cartoon 3D short. Story mode, one steady narrator (ElevenLabs "Henry"), no captions.

## Facts (with sources)
Web pages could not be opened directly from the build machine (network policy), so facts were checked through web
search summaries that quote these pages.

| # | Fact used in the script | Source |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Apollo 13 launched 11 April 1970; on 13 April 1970 (Houston time) oxygen tank no. 2 in the service module exploded, roughly 200,000 miles (~321,000 km) from Earth on the way to the Moon. | History.com "Apollo 13"; Britannica; NASA mission page |
| 2 | First words to Houston: Swigert, "Okay, Houston, we've had a problem here." Lovell repeated: "Houston, we've had a problem." | NASA Apollo Flight Journal (Day 3); Wikipedia "Houston, we have a problem"; ABC13 |
| 3 | "Houston, we have a problem" is the 1995 film's version (screenwriter William Broyles Jr. changed the tense); it was never said on the mission. | Wikipedia "Houston, we have a problem"; Mental Floss; Irish Times |
| 4 | The crew used the lunar module Aquarius as a lifeboat; its CO2 removal (lithium hydroxide) was sized for two people for about two days; it had to keep three alive for about four days. | Sky at Night Magazine; Wikipedia "Apollo 13"; NASA Flight Journal (Day 3 "lifeboat"). Note: some sources give 45 h or ~30 h for the LM canisters; the script says "two days". |
| 5 | Oxygen was sufficient; the danger was carbon dioxide building up. | NASA ALSJ "Apollo 13 LiOH Adapter"; History.com |
| 6 | Command module LiOH canisters are square; the LM's are round (cylindrical): not interchangeable. | NASA ALSJ "LiOH Adapter"; Smithsonian NASM "Conserving Creativity"; PopSci "Greatest Space Hack Ever" |
| 7 | Engineers in Houston (team of Ed Smylie) designed an adapter only from items on board. | Space.com obituary of Ed Smylie (2025); Hackaday; NASA ALSJ |
| 8 | Items: two CM LiOH canisters, plastic bags (from the liquid-cooling garments), an LM cue card / flight-plan cover as cardboard stiffener, suit hoses, grey duct tape, and a sock to plug the canister's bypass hole. | NASA Apollo Flight Journal Day 4 "Building the CO2 adapter"; Spacelog transcript (Swigert: "you need something like a sock to put in the bottom"); PBS NOVA; Smithsonian NASM |
| 9 | CAPCOM Joe Kerwin read the build steps to the crew by radio; ~48-60 min later Swigert: "Our do-it-yourself lithium hydroxide canister change is complete." It was nicknamed the "mailbox". | NASA Apollo Flight Journal Day 4; DLR "A carbon dioxide absorber to the rescue" |
| 10 | CO2 fell from ~15 mm Hg to 0.2 mm Hg ("almost nothing") after the adapter was installed. | NASA LiOH canister test report (1970); NASA Flight Journal Day 4 (crew: "0.2 on our CO2") |
| 11 | All three astronauts returned safely on 17 April 1970. | NASA "Apollo 13 crew returns safely to Earth" |

Not used: exact CO2 peak (sources say 15 or 25 mm Hg), exact LM design hours (30 h / 45 h / 2 days).

Visual note (not narrated): the "engineers with crew cuts and pocket protectors" are a 1970s style choice for our cartoon cast.

Analogy: a phone charger that doesn't fit the wall socket abroad (square filter, round holder).

## Script (v1, 258 words, ~70 s at 3.7 words/s)
| Beat | Line |
|---|---|
| Hook | Three astronauts were saved from suffocating in space by duct tape, a plastic bag and a sock. |
| You see | You see, in April nineteen seventy, Apollo thirteen is two hundred thousand miles from Earth, on its way to the Moon. Then, bang. An oxygen tank explodes. Alarms everywhere. And the radio crackles: "Houston, we've had a problem." |
| Bang | The main ship is dying. So the three astronauts squeeze into the lunar lander, a tiny craft built for two people for two days. They need it for three, for four days. |
| Lifeboat | Oxygen? Enough. The problem is what they breathe out. Carbon dioxide. With every breath, the meter creeps up. |
| CO2 | The main ship has spare air filters. But they're square, and the lander's holder is round. |
| Square vs round | It's like landing abroad and finding your phone charger doesn't fit the wall socket. Except up here, you don't run out of battery. You run out of air. |
| Analogy | Down in Houston, engineers with crew cuts and pocket protectors pile up only what the crew has on board. Plastic bags. Cardboard. Space suit hoses. A sock. And lots of duct tape. |
| Heroes on the ground | They invent a box that hooks the square filter to a round hose, and read the instructions up by radio, step by step. An hour later, the crew has built it: the mailbox. |
| The fix | The carbon dioxide drops to almost nothing. All three make it home. |
| Payoff | And that famous line, "Houston, we have a problem"? Nobody ever said it. The real words were "Houston, we've had a problem." Past tense. Thanks to a sock, it stayed that way. |
